# Reel script: Veteran Petroleum and transnational issue estoppel

Sources:
- The Russian Federation v Veteran Petroleum Ltd [2026] SGCA(I) 5 ("VP"), judgment 22 September 2026, delivered by Lord Mance IJ for a five-judge bench (Menon CJ, Chong JCA, Ang JCA, Mance IJ, French IJ).
- Background only: Merck Sharp & Dohme Corp v Merck KGaA [2021] 1 SLR 1102 ("Merck"), where the Singapore Court of Appeal first set out the test for transnational issue estoppel. VP is the main authority throughout and restates that test at [18]–[23].

Target: about 2:50 spoken at a normal pace. Each section gets a question card on screen while you speak.

---

## Hook (about 8 s)
**On screen:** Can a state make you fight the same case in every country?

**Say:** Russia lost on jurisdiction in the Dutch appeal courts, and again in England. In Singapore it argued: hear it all again from scratch. Here's why the Court of Appeal said no.

## Q1. What is issue estoppel and why do courts have it? (about 20 s)
**On screen:** 1. Issue estoppel / Decided once, decided for good

**Say:** Issue estoppel means once a court has finally decided an issue between two parties, the losing side can't reopen that same issue later. Why? Two reasons. Nobody should be dragged through the same fight twice. And litigation has to end somewhere.

**Card points:** final judgment on the merits · competent court · same parties · same subject matter · the issue was a necessary step in the decision
**Source:** VP [18], citing Lee Tat Development Pte Ltd v MCST Plan No 301 [2005] 3 SLR(R) 157 at [14]–[15]; operates through s 42 Evidence Act 1893 (VP fn 2). Rationale quoted at VP [19].

## Q2. What is transnational issue estoppel? (about 20 s)
**On screen:** 2. Across borders

**Say:** Transnational issue estoppel is the same idea, but the first decision comes from a foreign court. Singapore's Court of Appeal first set out the test in 2021, in Merck. The foreign judgment must be one Singapore can recognise: final, on the merits, from a court with jurisdiction over that party. Then the same parties, and the same issue.

**Card points:** recognisable foreign judgment · same parties · same issue · applied with extra care · origin: Merck (2021), then Deutsche Telekom (2024), now Veteran Petroleum
**Source:** VP [19]–[21] (quoting Merck and Deutsche Telekom)

## Q2b. Does the word "transnational" matter? (about 20 s)
**On screen:** Same doctrine, extra gatekeeping

**Say:** Does "transnational" really change anything? Less than Russia hoped. The elements are broadly the same as at home. What the label adds is a gate and some guardrails: the foreign judgment must be recognisable here, and the court weighs comity against its own role. Russia accepted Singapore's own rulings would bind it. The court asked why a foreign seat court's ruling should be any different, and found no good reason.

**Card points:** same elements (VP [21]) · plus recognition gateway · plus outer limits and comity · no special carve-out for states (VP [64])
**Source:** VP [21], [30]–[31], [37], [64]

## Q3. What makes an issue worth rehearing? Comity? (about 35 s)
**On screen:** 3. When can it be reheard?

**Say:** So when does an issue deserve a second hearing? The court balances comity, which is respect for other countries' courts, against its own job of guarding the rule of law in Singapore. It may refuse the estoppel where Singapore law requires the court to decide the point itself, where the foreign ruling only has effect in that country, where the defendant had good reason not to fight abroad, where the ruling breaks public policy or is perverse, or where truly new evidence emerges that couldn't have been found earlier. But simply disagreeing with the foreign court is not enough.

**Card points:** forum must decide it itself · territorially limited ruling · defendant had good reason not to contest · public policy or perverse · genuinely new evidence (Arnold) · always: must work justice, not injustice
**Source:** VP [21]–[23] (restating the limits from Deutsche Telekom and Merck), [60], [63]. Comity versus the court's rule-of-law role: VP [20]–[21].

## Q4. What does Veteran Petroleum add about state immunity? (about 30 s)
**On screen:** 4. State immunity is no escape hatch

**Say:** Veteran Petroleum adds this. Estoppel can apply even when a state claims immunity. Singapore still decides the law of immunity for itself. But the facts behind it, here whether Russia agreed to arbitrate under the Energy Charter Treaty, were already decided by the Dutch courts at the seat of the arbitration. Russia is bound by those findings. A state has no special right to relitigate the same facts in every country.

**Card points:** forum applies its own immunity law · seat-court findings of fact bind the state · no de novo rerun in each country · decided for seat-court rulings; other courts left open
**Source:** VP [28], [35], [51], [59], [63]–[66], [79]; State Immunity Act ss 3 and 11

## Q5. How do courts prevent an abuse of process? (about 25 s)
**On screen:** 5. Abuse of process

**Say:** Estoppel sits alongside abuse of process as tools to stop parties being vexed twice. In Veteran Petroleum, Russia raised a new point only at the oral hearing, based on a 2020 Russian court ruling. It had been available since 2020, was never put to the SICC, and Russia never tried to reopen it in The Hague. The court said it was neither appropriate nor just to let Russia rely on it for the first time on appeal.

**Card points:** estoppel and abuse of process: tools against relitigation (VP [19]) · Henderson v Henderson (VP [38]) · late new points refused (VP [76], [78])
**Source:** VP [19], [38], [67], [76]–[78]

## Q6. Why does the Russian element raise unique issues of repeat hearings? (about 30 s)
**On screen:** 6. The Russian twist

**Say:** That ruling is the Russian twist. After losing in The Hague, Russia amended its constitution, then obtained a ruling from its Constitutional Court, heard behind closed doors with only Russian state bodies heard, that provisional application didn't mean consent to arbitration. The court said that isn't ordinary jurisprudence. And Russia's approach, deciding immunity afresh in every country, would mean the same fight over awards worth around 60 billion US dollars being replayed court after court, with no end.

**Card points:** constitution amended after the Dutch loss · closed-door ruling, one side heard · same fight in the Netherlands, England, the US and Singapore · no finality
**Source:** VP [3]–[4], [57(f)], [59], [71]–[77]

## Close (about 10 s)
**On screen:** One fair fight at the seat. Then it's settled.

**Say:** So: one fair fight at the seat of the arbitration, and then it's settled. Should states get a second chance? Tell me in the comments.

**Small print on screen:** General information, not legal advice.

## Accuracy notes
- Russia first won at the District Court of The Hague (2016), then lost at The Hague Court of Appeal (2020) and the Supreme Court of the Netherlands. A fraud ground is still pending in Dutch cassation (VP [9]).
- Award amounts: about US$50 billion, US$1.8 billion and US$8.2 billion, plus compound interest and costs (VP [3]).
- The Court of Appeal decided this only for seat-court decisions. It said similar finality concerns may apply to other courts but left that open (VP [65]–[66]).
- VP does not label Russia's late reliance on the 2020 ruling as "abuse of process". It refused it as late, and said that even if admitted it would not disapply the estoppel (VP [78]). Keep the script's wording to "refused", not "abuse".
- Lee Tat [2005] is cited through VP [18]. Read Lee Tat itself before quoting it directly.
- Courts do decide immunity for themselves where a foreign ruling conflicts with basic immunity principles, for example Germany v Italy and the Ukrainian judgments against Russia (VP [28(c)], [50], [57]).
